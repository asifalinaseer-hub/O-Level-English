Core Understanding

What Does “Comprehension” Really Mean?

Comprehension is not just reading words. It is the ability to understand what is stated, what is suggested, what the writer wants the reader to notice, and what exact answer the question demands.

🔎

Explicit Meaning

This is the meaning clearly stated in the passage. A strong student locates the exact sentence, selects only the required detail and avoids copying unnecessary lines.

🧠

Implicit Meaning

This is suggested meaning. The answer is not always directly written. You infer from actions, reactions, tone, word choice, behaviour and the situation.

🎯

Precise Answering

Good answers are focused, short and accurate. They do not retell the story. They respond exactly to the wording of the question.

A* Method

The 5-Step Comprehension Answer Method

Weak students often read quickly and answer immediately. Strong students slow down, decode the question and select only what earns marks.

1

Read the Question Before Writing

First identify what the question is asking: a detail, a reason, an impression, a feeling, an explanation, or evidence. Never begin by copying the passage.

2

Underline the Command Word

Words such as identify, explain, why, how, what impression and own words tell you the type of answer expected.

3

Locate the Exact Passage Area

Use line references or keywords. Read before and after the target line because meaning often depends on context, not one isolated word.

4

Select, Do Not Dump

Examiners reward relevant selection. A long copied answer may contain the right phrase but still lose marks because it is unfocused.

5

Answer in Clear Exam Language

Use complete sense, precise vocabulary and controlled explanation. Where the question asks for your own words, change the wording while keeping the meaning accurate.

Question Language

Command Words Students Must Understand

Many wrong answers come from misunderstanding the question, not from misunderstanding the passage.

Identify

Find and state the exact answer. Usually short. Do not explain unless the question asks you to.

Explain

Make the meaning clear. Show the reason, implication or effect behind the answer.

Why

Give the cause, motive or reason. Look for clues in action, context and attitude.

How

Explain the method or process. In language questions, show how words create an effect.

Own Words

Do not copy the key phrase. Replace it with accurate equivalent meaning.

Impression

State what feeling, picture or idea the writer creates in the reader’s mind.

Evidence

Use a precise word, phrase or detail from the passage to support your point.

Attitude

Identify the writer’s or character’s feeling, opinion or viewpoint towards something.

Answer Formula

The C-I-E Method for Comprehension

Use this simple framework whenever a question requires more than a one-word answer.

C

Claim

Give the direct answer first. Do not begin with vague phrases. State the exact idea clearly.

I

Information

Refer to the relevant detail from the passage. This may be a situation, action, phrase or clue.

E

Explanation

Explain how the information supports your answer. This is especially important for inference, feelings, impressions and attitude questions.

Avoid These Mistakes

Why Students Lose Marks in Comprehension

These are the most common habits that stop students from reaching higher bands.

1. Copying Without Thinking

Copying may work only for very direct questions. For inference, own-word and explanation questions, copied material often shows weak understanding.

2. Writing Too Much

Longer does not mean better. In short-answer comprehension, extra information may make the answer unclear or contradictory.

3. Missing the Line Context

A word or action often makes sense only when read with the sentence before and after it.

4. Weak Own Words

Changing one or two words is not enough. Students must show the same meaning using fresh, accurate wording.

5. No Evidence for Inference

An inference must be based on a clue. A strong answer connects the clue to the implied idea.

6. Misreading Feelings

Students often choose a broad feeling such as “happy” or “sad” when the better word is more precise.

A* Vocabulary Bank

Precise Words for Feelings and Attitudes

Strong comprehension answers often depend on accurate vocabulary for emotion, viewpoint and attitude.

Anxious

Worried because something bad may happen.

Reluctant

Unwilling or hesitant to do something.

Resentful

Bitter because one feels unfairly treated.

Overwhelmed

Unable to cope because something feels too intense.

Suspicious

Not trusting someone or something.

Determined

Strongly committed to achieving something.

Admiring

Showing respect or approval.

Dismissive

Treating something as unimportant or not worth attention.

Weekly Practice Plan for Comprehension

This plan is practical for students preparing alongside school studies. The aim is consistent improvement, not random solving.

Day 1 Read one narrative passage. Identify setting, characters, conflict and mood.
Day 2 Practise five direct questions. Keep answers short and exact.
Day 3 Practise five inference questions. Link each answer to a passage clue.
Day 4 Rewrite copied answers into proper own-word answers.
Day 5 Practise feelings and attitude questions using precise vocabulary.
Day 6 Mark your answers: remove extra detail, vague wording and unsupported claims.
Teacher Tip: A student should not just ask, “Is my answer correct?” The better question is: “Which word, clue or line proves my answer?”
Model Thinking

How an A* Student Thinks While Answering

The best students do not guess. They prove every answer from the text.

?

Question: Why is the character nervous?

Weak thinking: “Because he is scared.” A* thinking: “What exactly shows nervousness? Is it his movement, speech, silence, hesitation, or the situation around him?”

✓

A* Response Pattern

The character is nervous because he hesitates before acting and appears unsure of what will happen next. This suggests fear and uncertainty, rather than simple dislike or anger.

★

Why This Is Better

It gives the feeling, links it to behaviour and explains the implication. It does not simply copy or guess.
